Keith Hume Fraser Reserve · 95 Swansea Rd, Montrose

Four pitches. Two grounds. One home for local sport.

Lilydale Montrose United and Montrose Cricket Club have together developed a master plan to transform the reserve's tired, poorly drained playing fields into a four-pitch football precinct and two full-size community cricket grounds — with a new pavilion, viewing deck and proper parking — while leaving the bushland untouched — and want feedback from football families, cricketers and neighbours alike.

The club

Two clubs, one growing home

Lilydale Montrose United fields approximately 430 players in 2026, and has been growing at 5–10% a year across junior, senior, men's and women's football.

That growth has already overflowed the reserve: the club regularly hires an additional ground on Quarry Rd, Lilydale just to fit preseason training and season fixtures. Every hired hour is demand that Keith Hume Fraser Reserve can't currently meet — and it's the clearest evidence for why two pitches need to become four. The master plan brings the club's football home to one ground, with capacity for the growth that's already happening.

Montrose Cricket Club — the Wolves — brings more than a century of local sport to the proposal: established in 1919, a founding member of the Ringwood and Districts Cricket Association, and today one of the largest and fastest-growing clubs in the Yarra Ranges with more than 400 members — nine junior teams, six senior men’s teams, two women’s teams, an All Abilities program, and the largest Cricket Blast program in metropolitan Melbourne. Around 75% of the club’s weekly cricket activity already happens at Keith Hume Fraser Reserve — it is the heart of the Wolves’ junior pathway, and the two full-size, protected cricket grounds in this plan are designed around exactly that program.

More about the clubs at lilydalemontrose.football and montrosecricketclub.org.au.

Why now

The grounds as they are

This isn’t a drainage complaint in the abstract. The photo below is the top pitch — the better of the reserve’s two — after ordinary July rain in 2026.

“This whole bottom pitch is as close to unmarkable and unplayable as I’ve seen in the 150+ pitches we mark.”

“The problem isn’t so much that it’s wet — the problem is there’s almost no grass on the top oval.”

John O’Donnell, pitch line-marking contractor — messages to the club, July 2026.
His practical advice for next week: switch the line colour from red to black, because black shows up better on bare dirt.

Mid-season, this is the playing surface — unable to drain, unable to recover between fixtures, and carrying more football than it was ever built for. It’s why the club already hires an overflow ground on Quarry Rd, and it’s what the master plan’s regraded, properly drained pitches are designed to end.

While the plan is decided

Three fixes that shouldn't wait

The master plan is the long game. These smaller items would make the reserve work better this season, and the clubs are pursuing them with council now:

Car park run-off

When the E2 car park was sealed, a raised edge was included — at the club's request — to keep stormwater off the grounds. It isn't working: most of the car park's run-off still drains straight onto the lower pitch, which now floods worse than before the works. Extending the edge or adding a collection drain would fix it.

Under-pitch drains

The existing drainage lines under the ovals need a proper professional clean-out, so the water that does land has somewhere to go.

Club room spouting

The club room spouting has failed, and roof water currently discharges straight onto the top pitch. Replacing it removes a constant source of waterlogging.

Got another quick win? Put it in the survey — this list is meant to grow.

The plan

What's proposed

All four pitches sit on one alignment on the Swansea Rd side of the watercourse, in two paired precincts. In winter each pair hosts football; in summer each precinct becomes one continuous cricket ground with a concrete wicket at its centre.

The pitches

Pitch 2 at 105 × 68 m and Pitch 3 at 100 × 68 m are the main grounds, aiming for 200 lux match lighting; Pitch 1 (88 × 60 m) and Pitch 4 (75 × 56.4 m) support them at 100 lux training/junior standard. Every goal has a minimum 4 m runoff, with fences pulled tight specifically to save the trees along Swansea Rd.

The pavilion

A 70 × 25 m building between the main pitches on a 3 m podium: six player change rooms, two referee rooms, first aid, and a canteen serving both grounds — topped by a rooftop viewing area sighting all four pitches, and a 20 × 20 m open deck at its northern end for outdoor table viewing. Modular build, following the award-recognised Aspendale Gardens pavilion model.

The waterways

The main watercourse is not moved at all. The only channel work in the whole scheme is a ~105 m naturalised realignment of the upper Montrose drain — gentle meanders replacing today's awkward right-angle jog, arguably leaving the channel better than it is now.

The bushland

The steep north corner stays exactly as it is: untouched bushland reserve. The car park sits on the already-disturbed old tennis ground, roughly 12 m off the nearest neighbour with the strip kept as vegetation.

Cricket

Two proper grounds — and a safety fix that's overdue

The cricket sizes are protected in the design: a 110 m boundary on the south ground and 100 m on the north. The football geometry was made to fit around the cricket, not the other way round.

The plan also fixes something the current ground gets badly wrong. The existing northern wicket was surveyed at a 65.5° bearing — which points its south-western batter directly into the setting summer sun, in the final hour of every 1pm–7pm fixture. That's the exact situation that halted an international match at Napier's McLean Park on safety grounds. The new layout eliminates it: both wickets are engineered to fully compliant bearings — 326° on the north ground and 325° on the south — keeping the low evening sun well clear of the batters' eyes, with the treed drain corridor screening the western horizon.

Put in the terms that actually matter for a ground that plays 1pm–7pm in summer: every wicket in the new plan keeps the low evening sun at least 60° from every batter's sight line; on the existing ground the best wicket allows it within 30°, and the worst points a batter directly into it.

As of drawing rev 9.45 that compliance is drawn in, not just planned: Pitch 1 shifts about 3 m south-west in Stage 3 to widen the pitch 1/2 gap, rotating the south wicket to 325° while holding the 1 m slab clearance to every pitch line. It has no effect on Stages 1 and 2, and final confirmation rides on the detailed site survey of the south-west drain clearance. A six-ground benchmark of measured district wicket bearings — including the best-performing ground in the council area — is in the project summary (PDF) below.

There's a second safety reason for how the wickets are placed, and it's a sombre one. Most shared grounds — including the reserve's existing ovals — carry their concrete wicket in the middle of the playing field under a seasonal cover, and over the years our players have taken hard falls onto it, including a few broken ankles. In July 2026 a young footballer tragically died at Lalor Recreation Reserve after striking his head on the covered concrete wicket in the middle of the ground, and councils across Victoria are now reviewing every covered in-field wicket. This plan removes the hazard instead of covering it: the concrete slabs sit between the pitches, never within one — at least a metre outside every pitch line, with the team shelters standing over them all winter. No football is played on or across concrete anywhere in the layout.

Wickets: concrete with carpet, between the pitches — never in the field of play

Access & parking

~220 spaces, zero new road connections

The old Montrose Terrace tennis club entrance at the reserve's south corner becomes the main car park access — reusing the existing crossover, its turning lanes, and the existing drain culvert. Only 75 m of new driveway is needed.

The ~6,200 m² car park sits on the former tennis ground, comfortably above the 120–180 spaces a venue this size typically needs at peak, with room in reserve. Both existing Swansea Rd entrances keep operating unchanged, and the reverse vending machine in the central car park stays exactly where it is. Match-day traffic parks within 100 m of the gate without crossing pedestrian routes between the pitches.

Delivery

Four stages, benchmarked on a proven project

The City of Kingston delivered almost exactly this project at Aspendale Gardens — four football pitches, two cricket ovals and a modular pavilion — jointly funded roughly 50/50 with the Victorian Government (~$9.1M total). This plan follows the same staged, co-funded model.

1Pitches 3 & 4, north cricket ground, wicket, fencing, drainage platform, north lighting — existing club rooms keep operating$2.5M – $3.0M

3Pavilion on its podium: enclosed building + viewing deck + rooftop (modular design-and-construct)$3.5M – $6.5M
4Pitches 1 & 2, south cricket ground, south lighting, drain naturalisation$2.0M – $2.2M
Indicative total$9M – $13M

*Aspendale-benchmarked planning figures — pre-survey, pre-quantity-surveyor, subject to escalation. The Stage 2 figure apportions the former combined Stage 2+4 allowance at standard at-grade parking rates (~$4,500–$6,000 per sealed space, including the new driveway and crossover); stages 2 and 4 together still total $3.0M – $3.5M. With a state co-funding partnership on the Aspendale model, council's share spreads across four budget years — with the car park brought forward as its own smaller stage — and each stage is independently useful.

And where Aspendale leaned on council and state alone, we believe KHFR's funding case is broader. This redevelopment fits squarely within Football Victoria's Facilities Strategy 2025–2035 — which reports Victoria has just one pitch for every 7,000 people against a recommended one per 5,000, with participation up 43% since 2022 — and a project that adds pitch capacity and keeps winter sport running should attract federal government funding as well. Council's contribution is the seed, not the whole bill.

Questions

Straight answers

Will the watercourse be affected?

Barely. The main watercourse through the reserve is not moved at all, and the lower Montrose drain stays exactly on its existing course. The single piece of channel work is a ~105 m naturalised realignment of the upper drain — designed with gentle meanders, replacing a right-angle jog — which requires Melbourne Water consent and is deliberately modest.

What about trees along Swansea Rd?

Tree saving drove the fence design: every behind-goal fence sits at the 4 m minimum specifically to pull the fenced footprint off the treed frontage — roughly 860 m² less clearing than earlier versions of this same plan. The steep north corner stays as untouched bushland.

Will lighting affect nearby homes?

Light spill toward the Swansea Rd dwellings and the northern bushland is a design criterion from the first layout, not an afterthought. The lighting serves winter football only — cricket is a summer daylight game and doesn't use it — and only the two main pitches carry the 200 lux competition standard.

Will sport stop during construction?

No — that's what the staging is for. Stage 1 builds the new north ground while the existing club rooms and pitches keep operating; Stage 2 adds the car park and driveway; only after the pavilion exists (Stage 3) does Stage 4 rebuild the top ground. Aspendale Gardens ran the same sequence successfully.

How would it be paid for?

On the Aspendale model: a state/council partnership (theirs split roughly $4.5M / $4.6M). Each stage is a smaller, independently useful ask, spread across budget years, with the pavilion's open deck already built in as a cost-management measure. And unlike Aspendale, the case needn't rest on council and state alone — the plan's fit with Football Victoria's Facilities Strategy 2025–2035 makes it a strong candidate for federal community-sport funding too.

Who developed this plan?

Lilydale Montrose United FC together with Montrose Cricket Club — with every dimension verified: pitch runoffs, cricket boundary buffers, waterway offsets and boundary clearances are all checked in a survey-grade coordinate model (MGA55), issued alongside the drawings and ready for council's surveyors. The plan is now out for feedback from the community.
